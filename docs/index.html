Search funds and ETA, in plain language

Leveraged buyouts, explained

A leveraged buyout is buying a company mostly with a loan that the company's own cash pays back. Private equity firms do it with billions. Someone like me, searching for one small company to buy, would do it once: a government-backed bank loan, a loan from the seller and my own savings. The math is simple. The hard parts are paying the right price, checking that the earnings are real, having cash on Day 1, keeping the people, and growing enough that the debt works for you instead of against you.

The page has three parts: my situation, what I did, and what I found. "The simple version" boxes give the everyday picture; panels for searchers and investors hold the formulas. The worked example is the illustrative company from Buying a business, explained, not a real deal.

By Wilson Wu, founder of Dubbs Capital: an operator who studies AI engineering and is searching for a small business to buy. Figures as of 7 November 2026.

Part 1: Situation

Why I want to buy a business

I want to buy one good small company and make it worth more, using what I know about running a business and about AI engineering. That second part is called value creation: making a company more valuable after you buy it. I'm an operator who studies AI engineering and is searching for a small business to buy, and this page is me learning out loud.

Some vocabulary first. Private equity is money invested in companies that don't trade on a stock exchange, usually through funds that buy companies, improve them and sell them a few years later. Buyout funds invested $904B in 2025, up 44%, according to Bain & Company. A leveraged buyout, or LBO, is buying a company mostly with borrowed money and letting the company's own cash repay the loan. Leverage is finance-speak for debt.

Take the illustrative company on this page. It earns $1.0M of EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of yearly operating profit) on $5.0M of revenue, and in the example it sells for $4.0M. With $150K of fees and a $100K cash cushion on top ($250K in all), a bank loan pays $3.83M, the seller lends $213K and the buyer puts in $213K.

The simple version. Picture buying a rental house. You put a little money down, the bank lends the rest, and the tenants' rent pays the mortgage. Years later the loan is smaller and more of the house is yours. An LBO is a rental house where the tenants are the company's customers. In the illustrative example, the company makes about $800K a year for its lenders, the loan payment is about $600K, and about $200K is left over.

A search fund is when one person, the searcher, raises money from investors to pay for the hunt for a single company, then buys it and runs it as CEO. ETA, short for entrepreneurship through acquisition, is the wider idea: buy a business that already works instead of starting one from scratch. Searchers who pay for the hunt themselves run a self-funded search. That's my path.

Why now? In a March 2026 Chase survey of about 1,000 US small-business owners, 40% planned to retire within 10 years, 70% had no formal succession plan or were only in early-stage planning, and just 8% felt fully ready to hand over ownership (JPMorgan Chase). Buyers are getting choosier. BizBuySell counted 2,132 businesses that changed hands in Q3 2026 (closings that brokers reported to it), 7% fewer than a year earlier, at a median price of $350,000, up 17%, and its president said buyers lean toward businesses that are easy to finance (BizBuySell). Many of those buyers look like me: people leaving salaried jobs made up 42% of buyers on BizBuySell, about double the 2021 share (The New York Times, "In Search of a Boring Business", 12 March 2025).

My weak spot is the equity, the money that sits beneath the loan. A bank judges a real company with real cash flow, and that makes sense to me. Raising money from investors is a different skill, and I'm still learning it. I wrote up the equity side in Buying a business, explained; this page zooms out to the whole deal.

So what kind of company would I actually want to own?

What to buy

The company I want is steady and a little boring: customers need it every year, the owner is ready to hand it over, and the price leaves room for its cash flow to carry the loan. Exciting is for startups; a loan wants predictable. Before I look at the price, I check a buy box with three parts: the customers, the company and the deal.

RSM US lists what makes a company worth more to the next owner: customers spread across many accounts, recurring earnings and leaders who don't lean on the owner. Many small competitors are a plus for me, too: they're the neighbors I might buy later. For the deal, the owner has to want out (remember the 70% with no formal succession plan or only early-stage planning), and the price has to pass the bank's test. Lenders use the DSCR, the debt service coverage ratio: cash available for loan payments divided by the payments. One law firm reads the new rules of the SBA (the US Small Business Administration, which guarantees part of many small-business loans) as raising the minimum for most purchase loans to 1.25x, from 1.15x (PilieroMazza). Confirm with your lender.

I'm testing two industries against the box; my first explainer has the full version.

  1. Electrical contractors that maintain critical power for hospitals, labs and plants, with data centers as upside. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics projects electrician jobs to grow 9% from 2025 to 2035, much faster than average, with about 72,700 openings a year, and counts data centers among the reasons (BLS).
  2. Decorated apparel: screen printing and embroidery for schools, teams and companies. Some of it comes on multi-year contracts: one state university put its athletic apparel and uniforms out to bid for 5 years, plus two optional 1-year extensions, and required both screen printing and embroidery (Salem State University).

Data centers can feel like a taboo topic right now, with as much fear as hype. My honest read is that the long commitments mostly sit on the power side, not with the contractors. Meta's January 2026 nuclear agreements, for example, support up to 6.6 GW (gigawatts, a unit of power) of clean energy by 2035 (Meta). Maintenance for hospitals, labs and plants should pay with or without an AI boom, so data centers are my upside, not my bet.

Buying the right company is half the job. The other half is making it better.

How I would grow it

An LBO makes money through three engines, growing the earnings, paying down the loan and selling at a better multiple, and it carries one big risk: leverage also works in reverse. A multiple is the price divided by a year of earnings, so 4.0x on $1.0M of EBITDA is $4.0M.

In the illustrative plan, a scenario and not a forecast, earnings would grow 7% a year and the company would sell in year 6 at 4.5x. Growth would add $2.00M of value, the higher multiple $0.75M, and $1.84M of the loan would get paid down along the way. Selling at a higher multiple than you paid is called multiple expansion, and I wouldn't count on it: in 2025 it explained just 8% of private equity value creation in Gain's sample of deals that made money, against 75% for revenue growth (Gain). Bain's midyear report says funds now need 10% to 12% yearly EBITDA growth to plan on a 2.5x return, about double the 5% of a decade ago (The Consulting Report).

My levers would be pricing, a real sales process, add-on acquisitions (buying a smaller company in the same field and folding it in) and AI-run operations. The value-creation and exit sections show each one, including the risk: in the same illustrative model, if earnings fall 35% and stay there, the buyer's cash is gone.

That's the plan on paper. Next: what I actually did about it.

Part 2: Task and action

The task

My task had two jobs: find a good company at a fair price, and pay for it without betting everything. The first is a search problem. The second is math, which turned out to be the friendlier of the two. Here's what I did:

  • I joined Searchfunder, an online community for people buying small companies (members only), and swapped notes with other searchers.
  • I run a self-funded search, paying my own way instead of raising investor money up front.
  • I talked with sellers, business brokers and bankers about how deals really get done.
  • I built the illustrative model behind this page; the code is on GitHub, so you can check my math.
  • I read the news and a lot of social media, to hear what people think of all this.

Step one was learning that there's more than one way to do it.

Four ways to buy a company

The LBO math is the same at every size; what changes is who pays for the search, who owns what, and who runs the company afterward.

  • Traditional search fund. Investors pay for the search, about $400K to $500K, and the searcher ends up owning roughly a quarter to a third of the company after an 18 to 30 month hunt, per one SBA lending marketplace (GoSBA Loans).
  • Self-funded search. The searcher spends about $100K to $200K of their own money, buys with an SBA loan plus equity, can keep up to 100% and often buys within 12 to 24 months, per the same source.
  • Independent sponsor. No fund at all: the sponsor finds and signs a deal first, then raises money for that one deal (Revision Legal).
  • Private equity fund. Institutions invest in a fund that buys many companies. In GF Data's sample of private equity-backed deals, Q2 2026 buyouts averaged 7.0x the past year's adjusted EBITDA (ACG), and funds now hold companies about 7 years on average at exit (Bain & Company).

Does it work? Stanford's 2026 Search Fund Study tracked more than 850 US and Canadian search funds through 2025. 58% ever bought a company, typically after about 20 months; the median price of 2024 to 2025 deals was $16M; and pooled returns were a 33.9% IRR and 4.75x the money invested (Stanford GSB). IRR, the internal rate of return, is the yearly return on money, counting when it goes in and when it comes out.

Now the honest caveats. Those are traditional search funds, with deals far bigger than the illustrative company, and one law firm's summary says Stanford's 2024 edition did not evaluate self-funded searches (Fredrikson & Byron). Pooled returns add up all the money invested, so a few big winners can pull the average up. It's also getting harder: according to one advisory firm's summary of the Stanford study, 48% of the 2021 to 2024 funds had bought a company, against 86% of the 2007 to 2010 funds. Searchers sign about 2.5 LOIs (letters of intent: written offers that lay out the price and main terms) for every deal that closes (Calder Group). Outside the US and Canada, IESE found that 73% of finished searches bought a company, and of the exits, 38 made money and 11 lost money (IESE).

Whichever path you choose, the money stacks up the same way.

The capital stack, visualized

The price sets the loan, the loan sets the yearly payment, and the payment has to fit inside the company's cash with room to spare, so the bank's test caps what you can pay.

A capital stack is every source of money in a deal, drawn as layers from the safest at the bottom to the riskiest on top. Here is the illustrative company at 4.0x, from the bottom up:

  1. SBA 7(a) loan: $3.83M, 90% of the $4.25M total cost. The 7(a) is the SBA's main loan program: a bank lends, and the SBA guarantees part of it. It's senior debt, meaning it gets repaid first. A single 7(a) loan tops out at $5M (SBA), and since 4 July 2026 a borrower can combine 7(a) and 504 loans for up to $10M (SBA); confirm with your lender. The model assumes 9.75% over 10 years: the prime rate, the benchmark banks use to price many loans, was 7.00% (Federal Reserve), and the extra 2.75 points are my assumption.
  2. Seller note on full standby: $213K, 5%. A seller note means the seller lends part of the price; full standby means no payments while the SBA loan is outstanding. In the model it grows at 6% a year and is repaid when the company is sold.
  3. Buyer cash: $213K, 5%. With the seller note, this forms the equity injection, the money the buyer's side puts in beneath the loan. One law firm, Starfield & Smith, reads the new SBA rulebook (SOP 50 10 8.1) as requiring 10% of total project costs for an initial acquisition (Starfield & Smith), and PilieroMazza reads seller notes and other standby debt as capped at half of that minimum (PilieroMazza). As one SBA loan broker reads the new rules, at least half of the 10% must be the buyer's own money. Confirm with your lender.
  4. Investors: none at this price. They show up when the price rises.

The top of the stack takes the first loss. When lenders took over Pluralsight in 2024, Vista's roughly $4B of equity was essentially wiped out (The New York Times, DealBook, "Private Equity's Private Credit Problem", 12 March 2026). Same physics, smaller numbers.

Now the bank's test. The company makes $800K a year for its lenders and the payment is $600K, so the DSCR is 1.33x: a pass, with $200K to spare.

Try it: drag the price to 4.5x. The payment climbs to $671K, the DSCR falls to 1.19x, and the test fails. At 5.5x the DSCR is 0.99x, and the $5.18M loan would also go past the $5.0M limit on a 7(a) loan. The highest price that passes is $4.28M, or 4.28x. Then pick 5.0x and switch on investors: $647K of their money brings the DSCR back to 1.25x, and the buyer keeps 74.1%. That split follows the step-up assumption in my first explainer, which also covers how searchers raise this layer. All illustrative, and the 1.25x test and the 7(a) limit are one reading of the SBA rules; confirm with your lender. The takeaway: a lower price can do the job of outside money.

For searchers and investors: the formulas and the fine print
  • CFADS (cash flow available for debt service) = EBITDA x 90% (after cash taxes of 10%) minus upkeep of 2% of revenue = $900K minus $100K = $800K.
  • Project cost = price + $150K of fees + a $100K cash cushion = $4.25M at 4.0x. Buyer cash = 5% of it, seller note = 5%, SBA loan = the rest, minus any investors.
  • Yearly payment = 12 x L x r / (1 minus (1 + r) to the power of minus n), with r = 9.75% / 12 and n = 120 months: $600K on a $3.83M loan.
  • DSCR = CFADS / yearly payment = $800K / $600K = 1.33x. The biggest loan that passes has a payment of CFADS / 1.25 = $640K, about $4.08M of loan; investors fill whatever the price needs above that.
  • The model ignores the SBA guaranty fee and any lender fees beyond the $150K of closing costs it assumes. SOP 50 10 8.1 takes effect on 1 October 2026 (SBA). PilieroMazza says it applies to loans that get an SBA loan number on or after that date, while Starfield & Smith says loans approved on or after it, so ask your lender which rules cover your loan. Starfield & Smith also reads it as capping standby debt, seller debt and small outside investors without control at half of the 10% together. On the loan broker's reading, a seller note counts toward the equity only on full standby, and investor money inside the 10% can receive only tax distributions until the loan is repaid. Confirm with your lender.
  • For scale: the SBA approved 56,130 7(a) loans worth $32.4B in fiscal 2026, a year that included a 43-day federal shutdown that halted SBA lending (SBA), after 77,600 worth $37B in fiscal 2025 (SBA). In GF Data's sample of private equity-backed deals, new platform buyouts carried total debt of 2.9x EBITDA in Q2 2026, with senior debt priced at 7.8% (ACG).

So the math puts a ceiling on the price. Next question: where do you find a company to put under it?

Where deals come from

The size of the company decides who sells it: business brokers handle small companies, M&A advisors the middle and investment banks the largest, while marketplaces and direct outreach to owners cut across sizes.

The sell side is the seller and the advisors working for them; the buy side is the buyer and theirs. A business broker sells small, owner-run companies for a commission. An M&A (mergers and acquisitions) advisor runs a more formal sale for mid-sized companies, and an investment banker advises on the largest, often through auctions. A proprietary deal is one you find yourself, before the owner hires anyone to sell.

Size also sets the price. In a Q2 2026 survey of 255 advisors by the IBBA and M&A Source, reported multiples rose from 2.0x SDE for companies worth under $500K to 3.1x at $1M to $2M, then 4.0x EBITDA at $2M to $5M and 5.8x at $5M to $50M (IBBA). SDE, seller's discretionary earnings, is profit plus the owner's own pay and perks, the usual yardstick for owner-run businesses. The illustrative company, at $4.0M, would sit in the band where the reported multiple was also 4.0x. Sellers pay the advisors: business brokers typically charge 8% to 12% of the price, often with a $25K to $75K minimum, and M&A advisors typically charge less as deals grow, down to 2% to 5% on $25M to $100M, per one 2026 guide (CT Acquisitions).

Each channel asks something different of a buyer. Broker listings reward buyers who are prepared and financeable. Advisors run formal processes: 87% of $5M-plus deals drew 3 or more offers (IBBA and M&A Source). Marketplaces such as BizBuySell are easy to browse, and BizBuySell's president said buyers are getting more selective (BizBuySell). Direct outreach is slow, but with 70% of owners lacking a formal succession plan or only in early-stage planning (Chase), many owners haven't been asked yet.

Once a seller is ready, the sale runs on a fairly standard track.

How a sale runs

A sale is a funnel that narrows step by step, from a short teaser to the closing, and it usually takes months. One firm puts a typical sale at 6 to 12 months in all (BD Emerson); the IBBA and M&A Source survey found deals averaged 6 to 10 months on Main Street and 11 to 12 in the lower middle market (press release).

The steps, with BD Emerson's typical times:

  1. The seller gets ready: 2 to 4 months.
  2. Teaser, NDA and CIM: 4 to 8 weeks. The teaser is a short summary, usually one or two pages, without the company's name. Interested buyers sign an NDA, a non-disclosure agreement to keep what they learn private, and receive the CIM, the confidential information memorandum: the seller's detailed booklet on the company.
  3. IOIs and management meetings: 4 to 8 weeks. An IOI, or indication of interest, is a non-binding price range that earns you time with the owner.
  4. The LOI, which usually starts exclusivity: a period when the seller agrees not to negotiate with anyone else. Exclusivity plus confirming diligence usually takes 60 to 90 days (BD Emerson), with documents shared in a data room, an online folder for the buyer to check.
  5. Diligence and the purchase agreement: 6 to 12 weeks. Alongside run the quality of earnings review (an accountant's check that the earnings are real), about 4 to 6 weeks (BD Emerson), and the SBA loan, typically 30 to 90 days from application to closing (Surge Business Law).
  6. Closing: 2 to 6 weeks.

Remember those 2.5 signed LOIs for every closed deal: plenty of offers fall out of this funnel after the handshake. That's the map. Part 3 is what it takes to walk it.

Part 3: Result

Meeting sellers, building the team

Deals are made by people: the seller has to trust you with their life's work, and you need your own team in place before you sign an LOI. Only 8% of owners in the Chase survey felt fully ready to hand over ownership (JPMorgan Chase), so early meetings are as much about trust as about numbers.

Sellers also want to know you can close. Among failed LOIs, valuation came up 45% of the time and investor support 40%, according to one advisory firm's summary of the Stanford study (Calder Group). And they like cash: in the IBBA survey, 83% to 92% of the price was paid at closing (IBBA). A buyer with a lender lined up and a clear plan for the equity is easier to trust.

The questions I'd bring to a founder:

  • Why sell, and why now?
  • Which customers would you hate to lose, and why do they stay?
  • If you were away for a month, what would break first?
  • How involved would you like to be after the sale?

Each side brings a small team. The seller has a business broker or M&A advisor, a lawyer and an accountant. On my side: the SBA lender, an M&A lawyer, a quality of earnings accountant, an insurance broker, a tax advisor and a mentor who knows the industry. The seller pays the broker, typically 8% to 12% of the price on Main Street deals (CT Acquisitions). The buyer pays for the checks: one CPA firm's 2026 price list puts a limited-scope quality of earnings review at about $20K to $35K for a company worth under $5M (BD Emerson). The illustrative model sets aside $150K of fees for that review, the lawyers and the lender.

The simple version. It's like buying a house from the family who built it. They want to know you'll look after it, so you show up prepared and kind. And before you make an offer, you line up your own inspector, lawyer and lender, because the seller's people work for the seller.

The first person I'd call is the accountant who checks the earnings.

Quality of earnings

A quality of earnings review, or QoE, checks that the earnings you're paying for are real and will repeat, because every dollar that isn't costs you the multiple. At 4.0x, $1 of earnings that isn't real costs $4 at the closing table.

An audit asks whether the financial statements follow accounting rules. A QoE asks the buyer's question: will these earnings show up again next year? It tests the add-backs, the working capital and the proof of cash, meaning whether bank deposits match reported sales (BD Emerson). An add-back is an expense the seller says a new owner won't have, such as the owner's personal car, added back to profit. What survives the review is normalized EBITDA: the earnings of an ordinary year under a new owner.

One CPA firm's 2026 price list puts a limited-scope QoE typically at $20K to $35K for a company worth under $5M, taking about 4 to 6 weeks. Books kept on a cash basis, counting money when it moves rather than when it's earned, can add 20% to 50% (BD Emerson). Two law firms read the new SBA rules as requiring a QoE when the price is $3M or more, and Starfield & Smith adds that it must be prepared for the lender's benefit (PilieroMazza; Starfield & Smith), so the illustrative $4.0M deal would need one. Confirm with your lender.

Here's the illustrative bridge. The seller's numbers show $1.25M of EBITDA with add-backs. Three common findings take it down: $80K of repairs called one-time that happen every year, $70K for relatives on payroll who do real work (someone would have to be paid to do it), and $100K of year-end jobs counted before they were finished. What holds up is $1.0M. At 4.0x, that's $5.0M versus $4.0M: $1.0M overpaid.

The simple version. The seller says the used car runs perfectly. A mechanic puts it on a lift and finds worn brakes. You don't have to walk away, but you pay less. A QoE is the mechanic for a company's profit.

For searchers and investors: what the QoE feeds

The QoE number flows straight into the bank test. One law firm reads the new rules as testing debt-service coverage at 1.25x on historical earnings, with owner-occupied real estate left out of the $3M threshold (Starfield & Smith). The working capital work also sets the peg, the agreed normal level of working capital, in the purchase agreement. Confirm with your lender.

Earnings are the biggest question, but not the only one.

The diligence checklist

Diligence answers one question: is there anything that should make me pay less, protect myself differently, or walk away? Due diligence is the buyer's investigation between the LOI and the closing, and it's where deals die: failed LOIs most often cite diligence findings, 79% of the time, according to one advisory firm's summary of the Stanford study (Calder Group).

One checklist splits the work into ten streams (BD Emerson):

  • Financial and QoE: are the earnings real, and will they repeat?
  • Commercial: will the customers stay?
  • Tax: is there a hidden tax bill?
  • Legal: which contracts, leases, lawsuits and licenses come along?
  • Technology: what systems run the business, and who owns the data?
  • Cybersecurity: has anything been breached?
  • Operational: how does the work actually get done?
  • Management and HR: who really runs the place?
  • Environmental: is there a problem in the ground or the permits?
  • Insurance: what has gone wrong before?

A typical data room holds 3 years of monthly financials, tax returns, major contracts, lawsuits, employment agreements, environmental reports and permits, and insurance loss runs, the insurer's record of past claims (BD Emerson). The SBA's own guide adds contracts and leases, licenses and permits, zoning and environmental issues (SBA).

Surprises after closing hurt. One buyer in a New York Times story learned after closing that the seller had taken the paper customer records, and that the person presented as general manager was really a dispatcher ("In Search of a Boring Business", 12 March 2025). In a small company I'd check three things first: how much of sales the top few customers make up, whether the place runs without the owner, and proof of cash.

The simple version. Before buying a house, you pay an inspector to check the roof, the wiring and the basement. Most findings mean a lower price or a repair credit. A cracked foundation means you walk.

If diligence comes back clean enough, it's time to sign.

How the deal closes

Closing is the day the buyer, the seller and the lender sign the purchase agreement and the money moves, after weeks of documents. Here are those documents in plain words:

  • APA or stock purchase. An APA, an asset purchase agreement, buys the company's assets. In a stock purchase the buyer inherits every liability, known and unknown, and contracts mostly stay in place. In an asset purchase the buyer picks which assets and liabilities to take and gets a tax benefit called a step-up, but key contracts must be transferred, which can slow the closing (Liff, Walsh & Simmons).
  • Reps and warranties: the seller's written statements of fact, such as having no undisclosed lawsuits.
  • Indemnification: the seller's promise to repay the buyer's losses if a statement turns out to be false.
  • Escrow or holdback: part of the price parked with a third party (escrow) or kept back by the buyer (holdback) for a set time, to cover those claims.
  • Working capital peg: the normal level of working capital the company must have at closing; the price moves up or down if the real level differs.
  • Earnout: part of the price paid later, only if the business hits agreed targets.
  • Non-compete: the seller's promise not to compete for a set time and area.

In the illustrative example, the SBA loan ($3.83M), the seller note ($213K) and the buyer's cash ($213K) add up to $4.25M. That covers the $4.0M price, of which the seller receives $3.79M in cash at closing plus the note; $150K of fees for the QoE, lawyers and lender; and a $100K cushion that stays in the company's bank account.

The simple version. It's a house closing. Everyone signs, the bank sends the mortgage money, you bring your down payment and the old owner hands over the keys. The seller note is the old owner agreeing to collect a slice of the price later.

For searchers and investors: escrows, insurance and SBA closing rules
  • SRS Acquiom's 2026 study of more than 2,300 private deals, many far larger than a search deal, found a median indemnity escrow in 2025 of 10% of deal value without reps and warranties insurance and 0.5% with it. Earnouts appeared in 24% of deals, and price adjustments after closing in well over 90% (Fasken via Legal 500).
  • Reps and warranties insurance costs about 3% of the coverage limit, and the limit is about 10% of deal value. One broker, Lockton, reports policies available at deal values as low as $10M, and smaller deals face minimum premiums and retentions (BD Emerson). In a deal smaller than that, I'd ask my lawyer how the buyer would be protected, for example with an escrow or a holdback.
  • One law firm reads the new SBA rules as requiring full underwriting for every acquisition: an independent valuation, a site visit and a test of historical cash flow (PilieroMazza). As one SBA loan broker reads the new rules, a seller note counts toward the equity only on full standby. Confirm with your lender.

Then the keys are yours, and so is the payroll.

Day 1 cash

On Day 1 you need cash for payroll before customers pay you, and who keeps the receivables decides how much. Receivables are what customers owe for work already done. Add inventory, subtract what the company owes its suppliers, and you get net working capital: the money tied up in running the business day to day.

If the receivables come with the business, under a working capital peg, customer payments keep arriving from the first week. In an asset purchase, though, the seller can keep the cash in the bank and collect what customers already owe. Then the buyer starts from zero, with payroll due every week.

Here's the illustrative example: weekly sales of $96K, weekly cash costs of $77K, loan payments of about $50K a month, and customers who pay about 45 days after the work. If the seller keeps the receivables, no customer cash arrives until week 8, and the balance bottoms out about $500K below zero. Counting the $100K cushion, the buyer needs about $600K of cash or credit in total, nearly 3 times the buyer's own $213K. If the receivables come with the business, cash never dips below the $100K cushion.

To cover the gap, negotiate the peg so the receivables come with the business, or finance working capital alongside the 7(a). One SBA lender's page says closing costs and working capital can be included in the financing, and its example deals pair a 7(a) loan with a $350K line of credit (a loan you draw on when needed and repay as customers pay) or a $350K SBA Express loan (Byline Bank). One SBA lending marketplace suggests building 3 to 6 months of working capital into the SBA request, a rule of thumb rather than an SBA rule (GoSBA Loans). Confirm with your lender.

My Day 1 list:

  • Open operating, payroll and collection accounts, then run a test deposit and payment (BD Emerson).
  • Run payroll in parallel with the old system before switching over (BD Emerson).
  • Have insurance in force from the moment of closing, and transfer or reissue licenses and permits.
  • Tell employees first, in person, then key customers and suppliers.

The simple version. You buy a lemonade stand whose neighbors pay their tabs at the end of the month. If the old owner keeps this month's tabs, you still buy lemons and cups for 4 weeks before any money comes in. That gap is your Day 1 bill.

For searchers and investors: the Day 1 math

Weekly sales = $5.0M / 52, about $96K. Weekly cash costs = ($5.0M of revenue minus $1.0M of EBITDA) / 52, about $77K. The monthly loan payment = $600K / 12, about $50K, paid in weeks 5, 9 and 13. With customers paying about 45 days after the work, the first new customer cash lands in week 8. The low point is about $500K below zero in week 9, so the cash needed is the $100K cushion plus about $500K. Illustrative, with flat weekly sales.

Cash is one Day 1 problem. Knowing what you're doing is the other, and that's where the seller comes in.

How long the seller stays

In my plan, the seller hands over for a few months and then stays on call; one law firm reads the new SBA rules as letting a seller stay on as a consultant for up to 24 months after the sale, up from 12 (Starfield & Smith). Confirm with your lender.

That help is called transition services: introductions, training and answers that the seller agrees, in the deal documents, to provide after closing.

An example plan, not a rule: in weeks 1 to 4 the seller works alongside me every day; in months 2 to 6, part time; after that, on call. The handover covers four things: customers (in-person introductions, biggest accounts first), suppliers (contacts and terms), staff (who does what, and who holds the relationships) and know-how (how jobs get priced, the quirks of the equipment, the unwritten rules).

UVA's Darden School advises new owners to learn the business before changing it (UVA Darden). I'd keep a list of things to fix and leave most of it alone for the first few months.

The seller note helps here. In the illustrative deal, the seller's $213K note grows at 6% a year and is repaid only when the company is sold, so the seller has a reason to want the business to thrive long after the keys change hands.

The simple version. It's like taking over a friend's paper route. The first week you ride along. Then you ride alone while they wave from the porch. For a while after that, you can still call them when a house says the paper never came.

Once the handover is underway, the real work starts: making the company better.

What Dubbs Capital brings

Dubbs Capital, the firm I'm building, would bring operating discipline plus AI engineering: find the slow, manual work in each department and replace it with custom agentic workflows and small web apps that the team actually uses. An agentic workflow is a chain of steps in which AI software reads information, decides the next step and does the routine part, such as drafting, sorting or reminding, while a person approves anything that matters.

Growth comes first, because growth is where value comes from now (remember Gain's 75%). The levers:

  • Pricing: check, job by job, whether prices have kept up with costs.
  • Sales process and CRM: customer relationship management software, so every lead gets a reply and a follow-up.
  • Website, local search, reviews and referrals: show up when nearby customers search, and ask every happy customer to say so.
  • Cross-selling: offer existing customers the other services.
  • Add-on acquisitions: buy smaller neighbors and run them on the same systems.

Then AI, one department at a time. Operations: a workflow drafts each quote from the job details for a person to approve. Sales: every lead gets a same-day reply and a reminder. Finance: invoice reminders go out on schedule and payments get matched. Accounting: transactions are sorted and reconciled for review, so the books close faster. Reporting: a daily one-page dashboard replaces the Friday spreadsheet. People: onboarding becomes a checklist app with training steps. Customer service: customers get automatic job updates instead of calling for status.

The evidence is encouraging but early. In a September 2024 survey of investors, Bain found nearly 20% of private equity portfolio companies had put generative AI to work with concrete results, and one named company, Cengage, reported cost cuts of 10% to 40% in narrow processes (Bain & Company). Big money is placing the same bet. Thrive Holdings owns more than 75 businesses, keeps 30 engineers embedded in them to find bottlenecks, and says it holds rather than flips (The Wall Street Journal). It agreed to buy ADT in a deal worth $15.3B including debt (Financial Times). The Journal story reports that its first AI uses there are unglamorous: fewer false alarms and better information for emergency responders. Long Lake, which says it uses frontier technology to speed up services industries, agreed to buy American Express Global Business Travel for about $6.3B in cash (Long Lake via Business Wire). AI labs are launching services firms with private equity partners too: Anthropic with Blackstone and Hellman & Friedman ($1.5B planned), OpenAI with TPG, Advent, Bain Capital and Brookfield (Financial Times).

Now the caution. General Catalyst put $1.5B of its latest fundraise into a strategy that builds AI-native software companies to buy established services firms, and TechCrunch's author warns the change may be harder than investors think (TechCrunch). US lawmakers have challenged plans for AI agents to run companies (Financial Times). And roll-ups fail on people. Renovo Home Partners combined family-owned remodelers into a company with 2,700 employees and $653M of revenue in 2022. Revenue fell to $449M in 2024, lenders wiped out the sponsor's equity in April 2025, and it liquidated that October with about 1,500 jobs lost; former employees blamed top-heavy central management and commission cuts that drove away the best performers (The New York Times, "How Private Equity's Vision for a Roofing Conglomerate Went Bust", 13 November 2025). My lesson: the tools serve the people, never the other way around.

So it's a bet, not a promise. I'd measure it with four numbers: hours saved, quote turnaround, days to get paid and days to close the books. A tool that doesn't move one of them gets switched off.

The simple version. Picture a busy kitchen where the cooks also answer the phone and write every ticket by hand. You don't replace the cooks. You give them a ticket printer and someone at the front desk, so they can cook. The AI tools are the ticket printer.

If all of that works, at some point someone else should want to buy the company.

Getting ready to sell

Preparing to sell starts on Day 1, and in the illustrative plan a sale in year 6 returns many times the buyer's cash, while a 35% drop in earnings wipes it out. These are scenarios, not forecasts. They count sale proceeds only and ignore fees and taxes.

  • If the plan works (earnings grow 7% a year; sale at 4.5x). EBITDA would reach $1.50M and the company would sell for $6.75M. After the $1.98M left on the SBA loan and $301K for the seller note with interest, the buyer would receive $4.47M: 21.0x the original $213K, an IRR of 66%. The bridge: $2.00M from earnings growth, $0.75M from the higher multiple and $1.84M of loan paid down.
  • Nothing improves (sale at 4.0x). Proceeds would be $1.72M, 8.1x, an IRR of 42%, almost all from paying down the loan.
  • Earnings fall 20% (sale at 3.5x). Proceeds would be $517K, 2.4x, an IRR of 16%, and the DSCR would sink to 1.07x along the way.
  • Earnings fall 35% (sale at 3.5x). The $2.28M sale would roughly equal the loans still owed, so the buyer's cash would be gone, and cash would fall $80K a year short of the loan payment (a DSCR of 0.87x).

The 21.0x is a MOIC, a multiple on invested capital: money back divided by money in. That's leverage working for you; the last case is leverage working against you. If a loan carries a personal guarantee, a promise to repay from your own assets, the damage can reach past the cash you put in, so ask your lender exactly what yours would cover.

The next buyer could be another searcher, a private equity fund, or a strategic buyer: a company in the same industry buying for fit. Bigger companies sell for higher multiples, from 2.0x SDE under $500K to 5.8x EBITDA at $5M to $50M in the IBBA survey (IBBA) and 7.0x in GF Data's sample of private equity-backed deals (ACG). Cleaner companies sell better too: RSM US points to depth of leadership, a spread of customers, recurring earnings and reliable reporting. So my readiness list is short: monthly books clean enough for the next buyer's QoE, a manager who could run things for a month without me, no customer big enough to sink a year, more recurring work, and written processes.

How long is a typical hold? Bain puts the private equity average at exit at about 7 years, up from 5 to 6 in 2010 to 2021 (Bain & Company). PitchBook data reported by The New York Times counted 33,575 unsold private equity-backed companies on 30 June 2026, up from 15,923 a decade earlier, with buyers and sellers still far apart on price ("Private Equity Is Stuck With 33,575 Unsold Businesses", 10 August 2026). PitchBook data in The Wall Street Journal shows nearly 24% of private equity-backed companies had been held at least 7 years, where firms once aimed for 3 to 5 (The Wall Street Journal). So I treat 5 to 7 years as a plan, not a promise.

The simple version. Fixing up a house to sell isn't only paint. A new roof and tidy paperwork make it easier for the next buyer's bank to say yes. Bigger, cleaner and easier to finance sells for more.

For searchers and investors: how the returns are computed

Sale price = exit multiple x year 6 EBITDA. Proceeds = sale price minus the SBA balance minus the seller note with 6% a year of interest. MOIC = proceeds / the buyer's $213K. IRR uses one cash outflow at closing and the proceeds in year 6. The model assumes every spare dollar after the loan payment goes back into the business, so returns count sale proceeds only, and it ignores the SBA guaranty fee, deal costs and taxes on the sale. Illustrative, not a forecast.

Numbers are one thing. What do people actually think of all this?

What people are saying

The news is about private equity's debt and AI roll-ups, and the online voices I read are wary of big private equity and warmer toward small owner-operators who keep the people. I read the Financial Times and The Wall Street Journal, plus The New York Times and The Economist through a university library, and public posts on X, Hacker News and Reddit (Reddit only through search results).

The news. Debt dominates. About $140B of below-investment-grade loans (loans to riskier, lower-rated companies), mostly private equity-backed, come due in 2028 and $160B in 2029, against about $60B in a typical 2-year stretch from 2023 to 2025, per PitchBook data in The Wall Street Journal; Bain's Hugh MacArthur warned of "keys tossed back to the bank" (The Wall Street Journal). The 10-year Treasury yield hit its highest level since 2002 and companies rated CCC, a very risky grade, paid about 17% (Financial Times). Fitch counts a 6.3% default rate in private credit, a 3-year high, mostly loans whose terms were changed rather than missed payments (Financial Times).

Returns get a hard look too. The FT reports US private equity trailed the S&P 500 by more than 3 percentage points a year over 5 years (Financial Times). Funds selling companies to their own new funds passed $100B in 2025, up from about $35B in 2019, which Alaska Permanent Fund's investment chief, Marcus Frampton, read as a sign of decay (The New York Times, "Investors Warn of 'Rot in Private Equity' as Funds Strike Circular Deals", 24 December 2025). The Economist notes software drew about one in three buyout dollars for a decade, and AI now threatens those companies ("Private-equity barons have a giant AI problem", 12 February 2026). Permira's co-chief executive sees a silver lining: lower prices and less competition may make today's deals some of the better ones (Financial Times). And the WSJ punctured the easy-money pitch for boring businesses: a small laundromat costs roughly $300K and takes a lot of daily hands-on work for a 24% average margin (The Wall Street Journal).

X. Of about 42 on-topic posts from 2026 in the top search results, roughly 12 were educational, 15 bullish or self-promoting, 6 skeptical, 1 mixed and 8 neutral, with Stanford's study as the shared reference; AI roll-ups drew 6 bullish posts out of 8 and no skeptical ones (X search). Codie Sanchez pitched buying a cash-flowing business with an SBA loan over chasing the next AI agent (X), and named private equity moving downmarket as her first worry (X). Greg Isenberg's post calling AI roll-ups a $5 trillion opportunity reached about 1.9M views (X). A macro analyst argued that when roll-ups raise prices in the trades, they leave room for new small competitors. And a meme about quitting a well-paid private equity job to roll up trades businesses, then discovering real work, reached about 418K views.

Hacker News, a forum for engineers and founders, barely mentions search funds: since 1 January 2025, 1 story on "search fund" (2 points) and no AI roll-up story above 8 points (Hacker News search). The biggest relevant thread (552 points, 555 comments) ran about three quarters against private equity: prices up, service down, debt loaded onto the company. The other quarter argued that retiring owners need buyers, that individuals can use SBA loans, and that leverage, not private ownership, is the problem (Hacker News). A thread on the debt wall leaned heavily negative (Hacker News). Of 13 comments pairing roll-ups with private equity, 10 were negative, while about 4 of 9 mentioning an SBA loan leaned positive (Hacker News search).

Reddit, from about 14 excerpts in search results, so treat it as anecdote: stories of buying instead of building (mostly tiny deals of $4K to $10K), love for boring businesses, warnings to check that tax returns match a broker's profit figure, and lots of financing questions. I saw no stories of debt stress.

What it means for how I'd run a company: keep the people and the service, borrow less than the bank allows, verify every number, and be the kind of small owner people root for.

The simple version. It's how people feel about restaurants. They distrust the faceless chain that bought their favorite diner and changed the recipe. They cheer the new owner who keeps the old cook and fixes the leaky roof.

So, after all of that, what have I actually learned?

What I have learned so far

I'm still early, but five lessons stand out, and each one comes with a number.

  1. Price sets everything. In the illustrative example, paying 4.0x passes the bank test at 1.33x, and paying 4.5x fails it at 1.19x (the 1.25x test is one reading of the SBA rules; confirm with your lender).
  2. Verify the earnings. $250K of earnings that aren't real would cost $1.0M at 4.0x (illustrative).
  3. Day 1 cash is a separate bill. If the seller keeps the receivables, the illustrative buyer needs about $600K of cash or credit, nearly 3 times their own $213K.
  4. The handover and the people decide a lot. One law firm reads the new SBA rules as letting a seller consult for up to 24 months, and I'd plan the handover around that (confirm with your lender).
  5. Leverage makes good outcomes great and bad outcomes total. In the illustrative plan, a sale in year 6 returns 21.0x the buyer's cash, while a 35% drop in earnings wipes it out (proceeds only; ignores fees and taxes).

Where I'm heading: keep searching, keep testing my two industries against the buy box, and get better at the part I find hardest, raising equity. If you've bought a company with an SBA loan, I'd love to compare notes.

Thank you to the Searchfunder community, and to the sellers, brokers and bankers who took my calls, for taking me from 0 to 0.5. Still a long way to go.

Glossary

  • Add-back: An expense the seller says a new owner won't have, added back to profit.
  • Add-on acquisition: Buying a smaller company in the same field and folding it into the first.
  • Agentic workflow: A chain of steps in which AI software reads information, decides the next step and does routine work, with a person approving anything that matters.
  • APA (asset purchase agreement): The contract for buying a company's assets rather than its shares.
  • Business broker: An advisor who sells small, owner-run companies for a commission paid by the seller.
  • Buy side: The buyer and the advisors working for the buyer.
  • Capital stack: Every source of money in a deal, drawn as layers from the safest at the bottom to the riskiest on top.
  • CFADS: Cash flow available for debt service, the cash a company has left for loan payments; $800K a year in the illustrative example.
  • CIM: Confidential information memorandum, the seller's detailed booklet about the company, shared after an NDA.
  • Data room: An online folder where the seller posts documents for the buyer to check.
  • DSCR: Debt service coverage ratio, cash available for loan payments divided by the payments; the illustrative example uses the 1.25x minimum that one law firm reads in the new SBA rules (confirm with your lender).
  • Earnout: Part of the price paid later, only if the business hits agreed targets.
  • EBITDA: Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of yearly operating profit.
  • Equity injection: The money the buyer's side puts in beneath the loan; one law firm reads the new SBA rules as requiring at least 10% (confirm with your lender).
  • Escrow: Part of the price held by a third party for a set time to cover claims against the seller.
  • ETA: Entrepreneurship through acquisition, becoming an entrepreneur by buying a business that already works instead of starting one.
  • Exclusivity: A period, usually starting at the LOI, when the seller agrees not to negotiate with other buyers.
  • Full standby: A seller note that receives no payments while the SBA loan is outstanding.
  • Holdback: Part of the price the buyer keeps back for a set time to cover claims against the seller.
  • Indemnification: The seller's promise to repay the buyer's losses if a statement in the purchase agreement turns out to be false.
  • Independent sponsor: A dealmaker without a fund who signs a deal first, then raises money for that one company.
  • Investment banker: An advisor who runs sales of larger companies, often as auctions.
  • IOI: Indication of interest, a non-binding price range a buyer offers early in a sale.
  • IRR: Internal rate of return, the yearly return on money, counting when it goes in and when it comes out.
  • LBO: Leveraged buyout, buying a company mostly with borrowed money that the company's own cash repays.
  • Line of credit: A loan you draw on when needed and repay as customers pay.
  • LOI: Letter of intent, a written offer that lays out the price and main terms. Most of it is not binding, but it usually starts exclusivity.
  • MOIC: Multiple on invested capital, money back divided by money in.
  • Multiple: The price divided by a year of earnings, so 4.0x on $1.0M of EBITDA is $4.0M.
  • Multiple expansion: Selling a company at a higher multiple than you paid for it.
  • NDA: Non-disclosure agreement, a promise to keep what you learn about a company private.
  • Net working capital: Receivables plus inventory minus what the company owes its suppliers: the money tied up in running the business.
  • Non-compete: The seller's promise not to compete with the business for a set time and area.
  • Personal guarantee: A promise to repay a loan from your own assets if the company can't.
  • Prime rate: The benchmark rate banks use to price many loans; 7.00% in the 6 November 2026 release from the Federal Reserve.
  • Private equity: Money invested in companies that don't trade on a stock exchange, often through funds that buy, improve and sell them.
  • Proprietary deal: A company you find yourself, before the owner hires anyone to sell it.
  • QoE: Quality of earnings review, an accountant's check that the earnings are real and will repeat.
  • Receivables: Money customers owe for work already done.
  • Reps and warranties: The seller's written statements of fact about the business in the purchase agreement.
  • SBA 7(a): The main loan program of the US Small Business Administration, in which a bank lends and the SBA guarantees part of the loan. A single 7(a) loan tops out at $5M (SBA), and since 4 July 2026 a borrower can combine 7(a) and 504 loans for up to $10M (SBA); confirm with your lender.
  • SDE: Seller's discretionary earnings, profit plus the owner's own pay and perks, used to price owner-run businesses.
  • Search fund: A way for one searcher to raise money from investors for the hunt for one company, then buy it and run it.
  • Sell side: The seller and the advisors working for the seller.
  • Seller note: A loan from the seller to the buyer for part of the price.
  • Senior debt: The loan that gets repaid first; in the illustrative example, the SBA loan.
  • Strategic buyer: A company in the same industry that buys another for fit, such as more customers or territory.
  • Teaser: A short summary of a company for sale, usually one or two pages, without its name.
  • Transition services: The help a seller agrees to give after closing, such as introductions and training.
  • Working capital peg: The normal level of working capital the company must have at closing; the price adjusts if the real level differs.
