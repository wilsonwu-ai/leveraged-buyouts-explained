## Why I want to buy a business {#situation}

**I want to buy one good small company and make it worth more, using what I know about running a business and about AI engineering.** That second part is called value creation: making a company more valuable after you buy it. I'm an operator who studies AI engineering and is searching for a small business to buy, and this page is me learning out loud.

Some vocabulary first. **Private equity** is money invested in companies that don't trade on a stock exchange, usually through funds that buy companies, improve them and sell them a few years later. Buyout funds invested $904B in 2025, up 44%, according to [Bain & Company](https://www.bain.com/insights/outlook-gaining-traction-global-private-equity-report-2026/). A **leveraged buyout**, or LBO, is buying a company mostly with borrowed money and letting the company's own cash repay the loan. Leverage is finance-speak for debt.

Take the illustrative company on this page. It earns $1.0M of EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of yearly operating profit) on $5.0M of revenue, and in the example it sells for $4.0M. With $150K of fees and a $100K cash cushion on top ($250K in all), a bank loan pays $3.83M, the seller lends $213K and the buyer puts in $213K.

A **search fund** is when one person, the searcher, raises money from investors to pay for the hunt for a single company, then buys it and runs it as CEO. **ETA**, short for entrepreneurship through acquisition, is the wider idea: buy a business that already works instead of starting one from scratch. Searchers who pay for the hunt themselves run a self-funded search. That's my path.

Why now? In a March 2026 Chase survey of about 1,000 US small-business owners, 40% planned to retire within 10 years, 70% had no formal succession plan or were only in early-stage planning, and just 8% felt fully ready to hand over ownership ([JPMorgan Chase](https://media.chase.com/news/local-snapshot-succession-survey)). Buyers are getting choosier. BizBuySell counted 2,132 businesses that changed hands in Q3 2026 (closings that brokers reported to it), 7% fewer than a year earlier, at a median price of $350,000, up 17%, and its president said buyers lean toward businesses that are easy to finance ([BizBuySell](https://finviz.com/news/404656/bizbuysell-releases-q3-2026-insight-report)). Many of those buyers look like me: people leaving salaried jobs made up 42% of buyers on BizBuySell, about double the 2021 share (The New York Times, "In Search of a Boring Business", 12 March 2025) <!-- src:nyt-boring -->.

My weak spot is the equity, the money that sits beneath the loan. A bank judges a real company with real cash flow, and that makes sense to me. Raising money from investors is a different skill, and I'm still learning it. I wrote up the equity side in [Buying a business, explained](https://wilsonwu-ai.github.io/buying-a-business-explained/); this page zooms out to the whole deal.

So what kind of company would I actually want to own?

## What to buy {#what-to-buy}

**The company I want is steady and a little boring: customers need it every year, the owner is ready to hand it over, and the price leaves room for its cash flow to carry the loan.** Exciting is for startups; a loan wants predictable. Before I look at the price, I check a buy box with three parts: the customers, the company and the deal.

<!-- FIG:fig-buybox -->

[RSM US](https://www.rsmus.com/insights/services/business-tax/how-owners-drive-business-value) lists what makes a company worth more to the next owner: customers spread across many accounts, recurring earnings and leaders who don't lean on the owner. Many small competitors are a plus for me, too: they're the neighbors I might buy later. For the deal, the owner has to want out (remember the 70% with no formal succession plan or only early-stage planning), and the price has to pass the bank's test. Lenders use the DSCR, the debt service coverage ratio: cash available for loan payments divided by the payments. One law firm reads the new rules of the SBA (the US Small Business Administration, which guarantees part of many small-business loans) as raising the minimum for most purchase loans to 1.25x, from 1.15x ([PilieroMazza](https://pilieromazza.com/five-sba-7a-changes-that-could-reshape-business-acquisitions)). Confirm with your lender.

I'm testing two industries against the box; [my first explainer](https://wilsonwu-ai.github.io/buying-a-business-explained/) has the full version.

1. **Electrical contractors that maintain critical power for hospitals, labs and plants, with data centers as upside.** The US Bureau of Labor Statistics projects electrician jobs to grow 9% from 2025 to 2035, much faster than average, with about 72,700 openings a year, and counts data centers among the reasons ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/construction-and-extraction/electricians.htm)).
2. **Decorated apparel: screen printing and embroidery for schools, teams and companies.** Some of it comes on multi-year contracts: one state university put its athletic apparel and uniforms out to bid for 5 years, plus two optional 1-year extensions, and required both screen printing and embroidery ([Salem State University](https://records.salemstate.edu/sites/default/files/rfp/2025-10/RFP%202026-03%20Athletics%20Apparel%20Supplies%20and%20Uniforms.pdf)).

Data centers can feel like a taboo topic right now, with as much fear as hype. My honest read is that the long commitments mostly sit on the power side, not with the contractors. Meta's January 2026 nuclear agreements, for example, support up to 6.6 GW (gigawatts, a unit of power) of clean energy by 2035 ([Meta](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/01/meta-nuclear-energy-projects-power-american-ai-leadership/)). Maintenance for hospitals, labs and plants should pay with or without an AI boom, so data centers are my upside, not my bet.

Buying the right company is half the job. The other half is making it better.

## How I would grow it {#scale}

**An LBO makes money through three engines, growing the earnings, paying down the loan and selling at a better multiple, and it carries one big risk: leverage also works in reverse.** A multiple is the price divided by a year of earnings, so 4.0x on $1.0M of EBITDA is $4.0M.

In the illustrative plan, a scenario and not a forecast, earnings would grow 7% a year and the company would sell in year 6 at 4.5x. Growth would add $2.00M of value, the higher multiple $0.75M, and $1.84M of the loan would get paid down along the way. Selling at a higher multiple than you paid is called multiple expansion, and I wouldn't count on it: in 2025 it explained just 8% of private equity value creation in Gain's sample of deals that made money, against 75% for revenue growth ([Gain](https://www.gain.ai/insight-reports/private-equity-value-creation-report-2026)). Bain's midyear report says funds now need 10% to 12% yearly EBITDA growth to plan on a 2.5x return, about double the 5% of a decade ago ([The Consulting Report](https://www.theconsultingreport.com/bain-says-market-shocks-have-slowed-private-equity-recovery/)).

My levers would be pricing, a real sales process, add-on acquisitions (buying a smaller company in the same field and folding it in) and AI-run operations. The value-creation and exit sections show each one, including the risk: in the same illustrative model, if earnings fall 35% and stay there, the buyer's cash is gone.
