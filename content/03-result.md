## Meeting sellers, building the team {#team}

**Deals are made by people: the seller has to trust you with their life's work, and you need your own team in place before you sign an LOI.** Only 8% of owners in the Chase survey felt fully ready to hand over ownership ([JPMorgan Chase](https://media.chase.com/news/local-snapshot-succession-survey)), so early meetings are as much about trust as about numbers.

Sellers also want to know you can close. Among failed LOIs, valuation came up 45% of the time and investor support 40%, according to one advisory firm's summary of the Stanford study ([Calder Group](https://caldergr.com/acquisition-search-is-getting-harder-takeaways-from-stanfords-2026-search-fund-study)). And they like cash: in the IBBA survey, 83% to 92% of the price was paid at closing ([IBBA](https://www.ibba.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/mp-highlights-q2-2026.pdf)). A buyer with a lender lined up and a clear plan for the equity is easier to trust.

The questions I'd bring to a founder:

- Why sell, and why now?
- Which customers would you hate to lose, and why do they stay?
- If you were away for a month, what would break first?
- How involved would you like to be after the sale?

<!-- FIG:fig-team -->

Each side brings a small team. The seller has a business broker or M&A advisor, a lawyer and an accountant. On my side: the SBA lender, an M&A lawyer, a quality of earnings accountant, an insurance broker, a tax advisor and a mentor who knows the industry. The seller pays the broker, typically 8% to 12% of the price on Main Street deals ([CT Acquisitions](https://www.ctacquisitions.com/ma-advisor-fees-how-much-do-they-charge/)). The buyer pays for the checks: one CPA firm's 2026 price list puts a limited-scope quality of earnings review at about $20K to $35K for a company worth under $5M ([BD Emerson](https://www.bdemerson.com/article/quality-of-earnings-report-cost)). The illustrative model sets aside $150K of fees for that review, the lawyers and the lender.

<div class="simple" markdown="1">
**The simple version.** It's like buying a house from the family who built it. They want to know you'll look after it, so you show up prepared and kind. And before you make an offer, you line up your own inspector, lawyer and lender, because the seller's people work for the seller.
</div>

The first person I'd call is the accountant who checks the earnings.

## Quality of earnings {#qoe}

**A quality of earnings review, or QoE, checks that the earnings you're paying for are real and will repeat, because every dollar that isn't costs you the multiple.** At 4.0x, $1 of earnings that isn't real costs $4 at the closing table.

An audit asks whether the financial statements follow accounting rules. A QoE asks the buyer's question: will these earnings show up again next year? It tests the add-backs, the working capital and the proof of cash, meaning whether bank deposits match reported sales ([BD Emerson](https://www.bdemerson.com/article/quality-of-earnings-report-cost)). An add-back is an expense the seller says a new owner won't have, such as the owner's personal car, added back to profit. What survives the review is normalized EBITDA: the earnings of an ordinary year under a new owner.

One CPA firm's 2026 price list puts a limited-scope QoE typically at $20K to $35K for a company worth under $5M, taking about 4 to 6 weeks. Books kept on a cash basis, counting money when it moves rather than when it's earned, can add 20% to 50% (BD Emerson). Two law firms read the new SBA rules as requiring a QoE when the price is $3M or more, and Starfield & Smith adds that it must be prepared for the lender's benefit ([PilieroMazza](https://pilieromazza.com/five-sba-7a-changes-that-could-reshape-business-acquisitions); [Starfield & Smith](https://starfieldsmith.com/2026/09/best-practices-change-of-ownership-transactions-under-sop-50-10-8-1-what-sba-lenders-need-to-know/)), so the illustrative $4.0M deal would need one. Confirm with your lender.

<!-- FIG:fig-qoe -->

Here's the illustrative bridge. The seller's numbers show $1.25M of EBITDA with add-backs. Three common findings take it down: $80K of repairs called one-time that happen every year, $70K for relatives on payroll who do real work (someone would have to be paid to do it), and $100K of year-end jobs counted before they were finished. What holds up is $1.0M. At 4.0x, that's $5.0M versus $4.0M: $1.0M overpaid.

<div class="simple" markdown="1">
**The simple version.** The seller says the used car runs perfectly. A mechanic puts it on a lift and finds worn brakes. You don't have to walk away, but you pay less. A QoE is the mechanic for a company's profit.
</div>

<details class="deep" markdown="1">
<summary>For searchers and investors: what the QoE feeds</summary>

The QoE number flows straight into the bank test. One law firm reads the new rules as testing debt-service coverage at 1.25x on historical earnings, with owner-occupied real estate left out of the $3M threshold ([Starfield & Smith](https://starfieldsmith.com/2026/09/best-practices-change-of-ownership-transactions-under-sop-50-10-8-1-what-sba-lenders-need-to-know/)). The working capital work also sets the peg, the agreed normal level of working capital, in the purchase agreement. Confirm with your lender.

</details>

Earnings are the biggest question, but not the only one.

## The diligence checklist {#diligence}

**Diligence answers one question: is there anything that should make me pay less, protect myself differently, or walk away?** Due diligence is the buyer's investigation between the LOI and the closing, and it's where deals die: failed LOIs most often cite diligence findings, 79% of the time, according to one advisory firm's summary of the Stanford study ([Calder Group](https://caldergr.com/acquisition-search-is-getting-harder-takeaways-from-stanfords-2026-search-fund-study)).

<!-- FIG:fig-checklist -->

One checklist splits the work into ten streams ([BD Emerson](https://www.bdemerson.com/article/private-equity-due-diligence)):

- **Financial and QoE:** are the earnings real, and will they repeat?
- **Commercial:** will the customers stay?
- **Tax:** is there a hidden tax bill?
- **Legal:** which contracts, leases, lawsuits and licenses come along?
- **Technology:** what systems run the business, and who owns the data?
- **Cybersecurity:** has anything been breached?
- **Operational:** how does the work actually get done?
- **Management and HR:** who really runs the place?
- **Environmental:** is there a problem in the ground or the permits?
- **Insurance:** what has gone wrong before?

A typical data room holds 3 years of monthly financials, tax returns, major contracts, lawsuits, employment agreements, environmental reports and permits, and insurance loss runs, the insurer's record of past claims (BD Emerson). The SBA's own guide adds contracts and leases, licenses and permits, zoning and environmental issues ([SBA](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/buy-existing-business-or-franchise)).

Surprises after closing hurt. One buyer in a New York Times story learned after closing that the seller had taken the paper customer records, and that the person presented as general manager was really a dispatcher ("In Search of a Boring Business", 12 March 2025) <!-- src:nyt-boring -->. In a small company I'd check three things first: how much of sales the top few customers make up, whether the place runs without the owner, and proof of cash.

<div class="simple" markdown="1">
**The simple version.** Before buying a house, you pay an inspector to check the roof, the wiring and the basement. Most findings mean a lower price or a repair credit. A cracked foundation means you walk.
</div>

If diligence comes back clean enough, it's time to sign.

## How the deal closes {#closing}

**Closing is the day the buyer, the seller and the lender sign the purchase agreement and the money moves, after weeks of documents.** Here are those documents in plain words:

- **APA or stock purchase.** An APA, an asset purchase agreement, buys the company's assets. In a stock purchase the buyer inherits every liability, known and unknown, and contracts mostly stay in place. In an asset purchase the buyer picks which assets and liabilities to take and gets a tax benefit called a step-up, but key contracts must be transferred, which can slow the closing ([Liff, Walsh & Simmons](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/united-states/corporate-commercial-law/stock-purchase-vs-asset-purchase-a-legal-perspective-for-buyers)).
- **Reps and warranties:** the seller's written statements of fact, such as having no undisclosed lawsuits.
- **Indemnification:** the seller's promise to repay the buyer's losses if a statement turns out to be false.
- **Escrow or holdback:** part of the price parked with a third party (escrow) or kept back by the buyer (holdback) for a set time, to cover those claims.
- **Working capital peg:** the normal level of working capital the company must have at closing; the price moves up or down if the real level differs.
- **Earnout:** part of the price paid later, only if the business hits agreed targets.
- **Non-compete:** the seller's promise not to compete for a set time and area.

<!-- FIG:fig-funds -->

In the illustrative example, the SBA loan ($3.83M), the seller note ($213K) and the buyer's cash ($213K) add up to $4.25M. That covers the $4.0M price, of which the seller receives $3.79M in cash at closing plus the note; $150K of fees for the QoE, lawyers and lender; and a $100K cushion that stays in the company's bank account.

<div class="simple" markdown="1">
**The simple version.** It's a house closing. Everyone signs, the bank sends the mortgage money, you bring your down payment and the old owner hands over the keys. The seller note is the old owner agreeing to collect a slice of the price later.
</div>

<details class="deep" markdown="1">
<summary>For searchers and investors: escrows, insurance and SBA closing rules</summary>

- SRS Acquiom's 2026 study of more than 2,300 private deals, many far larger than a search deal, found a median indemnity escrow in 2025 of 10% of deal value without reps and warranties insurance and 0.5% with it. Earnouts appeared in 24% of deals, and price adjustments after closing in well over 90% ([Fasken via Legal 500](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/canada/corporate-commercial-law/private-m%26a-deal-trends-to-watch-key-takeaways-from-srs-acquiom%E2%80%99s-2026-study)).
- Reps and warranties insurance costs about 3% of the coverage limit, and the limit is about 10% of deal value. One broker, Lockton, reports policies available at deal values as low as $10M, and smaller deals face minimum premiums and retentions ([BD Emerson](https://www.bdemerson.com/article/representations-and-warranties-insurance)). In a deal smaller than that, I'd ask my lawyer how the buyer would be protected, for example with an escrow or a holdback.
- One law firm reads the new SBA rules as requiring full underwriting for every acquisition: an independent valuation, a site visit and a test of historical cash flow ([PilieroMazza](https://pilieromazza.com/five-sba-7a-changes-that-could-reshape-business-acquisitions)). As one SBA loan broker reads the new rules, a seller note counts toward the equity only on full standby. Confirm with your lender.

</details>

Then the keys are yours, and so is the payroll.

## Day 1 cash {#day-one}

**On Day 1 you need cash for payroll before customers pay you, and who keeps the receivables decides how much.** Receivables are what customers owe for work already done. Add inventory, subtract what the company owes its suppliers, and you get net working capital: the money tied up in running the business day to day.

If the receivables come with the business, under a working capital peg, customer payments keep arriving from the first week. In an asset purchase, though, the seller can keep the cash in the bank and collect what customers already owe. Then the buyer starts from zero, with payroll due every week.

<!-- FIG:fig-day1 -->

Here's the illustrative example: weekly sales of $96K, weekly cash costs of $77K, loan payments of about $50K a month, and customers who pay about 45 days after the work. If the seller keeps the receivables, no customer cash arrives until week 8, and the balance bottoms out about $500K below zero. Counting the $100K cushion, the buyer needs about $600K of cash or credit in total, nearly 3 times the buyer's own $213K. If the receivables come with the business, cash never dips below the $100K cushion.

To cover the gap, negotiate the peg so the receivables come with the business, or finance working capital alongside the 7(a). One SBA lender's page says closing costs and working capital can be included in the financing, and its example deals pair a 7(a) loan with a $350K line of credit (a loan you draw on when needed and repay as customers pay) or a $350K SBA Express loan ([Byline Bank](https://www.bylinebank.com/?p=12073)). One SBA lending marketplace suggests building 3 to 6 months of working capital into the SBA request, a rule of thumb rather than an SBA rule ([GoSBA Loans](https://gosbaloans.com/?p=9640)). Confirm with your lender.

My Day 1 list:

- Open operating, payroll and collection accounts, then run a test deposit and payment ([BD Emerson](https://www.bdemerson.com/article/day-1-readiness-checklist)).
- Run payroll in parallel with the old system before switching over (BD Emerson).
- Have insurance in force from the moment of closing, and transfer or reissue licenses and permits.
- Tell employees first, in person, then key customers and suppliers.

<div class="simple" markdown="1">
**The simple version.** You buy a lemonade stand whose neighbors pay their tabs at the end of the month. If the old owner keeps this month's tabs, you still buy lemons and cups for 4 weeks before any money comes in. That gap is your Day 1 bill.
</div>

<details class="deep" markdown="1">
<summary>For searchers and investors: the Day 1 math</summary>

Weekly sales = $5.0M / 52, about $96K. Weekly cash costs = ($5.0M of revenue minus $1.0M of EBITDA) / 52, about $77K. The monthly loan payment = $600K / 12, about $50K, paid in weeks 5, 9 and 13. With customers paying about 45 days after the work, the first new customer cash lands in week 8. The low point is about $500K below zero in week 9, so the cash needed is the $100K cushion plus about $500K. Illustrative, with flat weekly sales.

</details>

Cash is one Day 1 problem. Knowing what you're doing is the other, and that's where the seller comes in.

## How long the seller stays {#transition}

**In my plan, the seller hands over for a few months and then stays on call; one law firm reads the new SBA rules as letting a seller stay on as a consultant for up to 24 months after the sale, up from 12** ([Starfield & Smith](https://starfieldsmith.com/2026/09/best-practices-change-of-ownership-transactions-under-sop-50-10-8-1-what-sba-lenders-need-to-know/)). Confirm with your lender.

That help is called transition services: introductions, training and answers that the seller agrees, in the deal documents, to provide after closing.

<!-- FIG:fig-transition -->

An example plan, not a rule: in weeks 1 to 4 the seller works alongside me every day; in months 2 to 6, part time; after that, on call. The handover covers four things: customers (in-person introductions, biggest accounts first), suppliers (contacts and terms), staff (who does what, and who holds the relationships) and know-how (how jobs get priced, the quirks of the equipment, the unwritten rules).

UVA's Darden School advises new owners to learn the business before changing it ([UVA Darden](https://news.darden.virginia.edu/roadmap-for-new-business-owners)). I'd keep a list of things to fix and leave most of it alone for the first few months.

The seller note helps here. In the illustrative deal, the seller's $213K note grows at 6% a year and is repaid only when the company is sold, so the seller has a reason to want the business to thrive long after the keys change hands.

<div class="simple" markdown="1">
**The simple version.** It's like taking over a friend's paper route. The first week you ride along. Then you ride alone while they wave from the porch. For a while after that, you can still call them when a house says the paper never came.
</div>

Once the handover is underway, the real work starts: making the company better.

## What Dubbs Capital brings {#value-creation}

**Dubbs Capital, the firm I'm building, would bring operating discipline plus AI engineering: find the slow, manual work in each department and replace it with custom agentic workflows and small web apps that the team actually uses.** An agentic workflow is a chain of steps in which AI software reads information, decides the next step and does the routine part, such as drafting, sorting or reminding, while a person approves anything that matters.

Growth comes first, because growth is where value comes from now (remember Gain's 75%). The levers:

- **Pricing:** check, job by job, whether prices have kept up with costs.
- **Sales process and CRM:** customer relationship management software, so every lead gets a reply and a follow-up.
- **Website, local search, reviews and referrals:** show up when nearby customers search, and ask every happy customer to say so.
- **Cross-selling:** offer existing customers the other services.
- **Add-on acquisitions:** buy smaller neighbors and run them on the same systems.

Then AI, one department at a time. Operations: a workflow drafts each quote from the job details for a person to approve. Sales: every lead gets a same-day reply and a reminder. Finance: invoice reminders go out on schedule and payments get matched. Accounting: transactions are sorted and reconciled for review, so the books close faster. Reporting: a daily one-page dashboard replaces the Friday spreadsheet. People: onboarding becomes a checklist app with training steps. Customer service: customers get automatic job updates instead of calling for status.

<!-- FIG:fig-ai -->

The evidence is encouraging but early. In a September 2024 survey of investors, Bain found nearly 20% of private equity portfolio companies had put generative AI to work with concrete results, and one named company, Cengage, reported cost cuts of 10% to 40% in narrow processes ([Bain & Company](https://www.bain.com/insights/field-notes-from-generative-ai-insurgency-global-private-equity-report-2025/)). Big money is placing the same bet. Thrive Holdings owns more than 75 businesses, keeps 30 engineers embedded in them to find bottlenecks, and says it holds rather than flips ([The Wall Street Journal](https://www.wsj.com/finance/investing/why-josh-kushners-firm-just-spent-7-6-billion-on-a-150-year-old-company-8bfdd9f8)). It agreed to buy ADT in a deal worth $15.3B including debt ([Financial Times](https://www.ft.com/content/f88e30a0-1252-4695-97b0-03da22e5fd42)). The Journal story reports that its first AI uses there are unglamorous: fewer false alarms and better information for emergency responders. Long Lake, which says it uses frontier technology to speed up services industries, agreed to buy American Express Global Business Travel for about $6.3B in cash ([Long Lake via Business Wire](https://www.financialcontent.com/article/bizwire-2026-5-4-long-lake-agrees-to-acquire-american-express-global-business-travel-the-worlds-largest-corporate-travel-platform-for-63-billion-with-support-from-general-catalyst-and-alpha-wave)). AI labs are launching services firms with private equity partners too: Anthropic with Blackstone and Hellman & Friedman ($1.5B planned), OpenAI with TPG, Advent, Bain Capital and Brookfield ([Financial Times](https://www.ft.com/content/df70bf20-e809-48d0-8485-f8f514a7fbc4)).

Now the caution. General Catalyst put $1.5B of its latest fundraise into a strategy that builds AI-native software companies to buy established services firms, and TechCrunch's author warns the change may be harder than investors think ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/09/28/the-ai-services-transformation-may-be-harder-than-vcs-think/)). US lawmakers have challenged plans for AI agents to run companies ([Financial Times](https://www.ft.com/content/d5021cdf-816e-4cb9-bbf9-e4ba816a004f)). And roll-ups fail on people. Renovo Home Partners combined family-owned remodelers into a company with 2,700 employees and $653M of revenue in 2022. Revenue fell to $449M in 2024, lenders wiped out the sponsor's equity in April 2025, and it liquidated that October with about 1,500 jobs lost; former employees blamed top-heavy central management and commission cuts that drove away the best performers (The New York Times, "How Private Equity's Vision for a Roofing Conglomerate Went Bust", 13 November 2025) <!-- src:nyt-renovo -->. My lesson: the tools serve the people, never the other way around.

So it's a bet, not a promise. I'd measure it with four numbers: hours saved, quote turnaround, days to get paid and days to close the books. A tool that doesn't move one of them gets switched off.

<div class="simple" markdown="1">
**The simple version.** Picture a busy kitchen where the cooks also answer the phone and write every ticket by hand. You don't replace the cooks. You give them a ticket printer and someone at the front desk, so they can cook. The AI tools are the ticket printer.
</div>

If all of that works, at some point someone else should want to buy the company.

## Getting ready to sell {#exit}

**Preparing to sell starts on Day 1, and in the illustrative plan a sale in year 6 returns many times the buyer's cash, while a 35% drop in earnings wipes it out.** These are scenarios, not forecasts. They count sale proceeds only and ignore fees and taxes.

<!-- FIG:fig-bridge -->

- **If the plan works** (earnings grow 7% a year; sale at 4.5x). EBITDA would reach $1.50M and the company would sell for $6.75M. After the $1.98M left on the SBA loan and $301K for the seller note with interest, the buyer would receive $4.47M: 21.0x the original $213K, an IRR of 66%. The bridge: $2.00M from earnings growth, $0.75M from the higher multiple and $1.84M of loan paid down.
- **Nothing improves** (sale at 4.0x). Proceeds would be $1.72M, 8.1x, an IRR of 42%, almost all from paying down the loan.
- **Earnings fall 20%** (sale at 3.5x). Proceeds would be $517K, 2.4x, an IRR of 16%, and the DSCR would sink to 1.07x along the way.
- **Earnings fall 35%** (sale at 3.5x). The $2.28M sale would roughly equal the loans still owed, so the buyer's cash would be gone, and cash would fall $80K a year short of the loan payment (a DSCR of 0.87x).

The 21.0x is a MOIC, a multiple on invested capital: money back divided by money in. That's leverage working for you; the last case is leverage working against you. If a loan carries a personal guarantee, a promise to repay from your own assets, the damage can reach past the cash you put in, so ask your lender exactly what yours would cover.

The next buyer could be another searcher, a private equity fund, or a strategic buyer: a company in the same industry buying for fit. Bigger companies sell for higher multiples, from 2.0x SDE under $500K to 5.8x EBITDA at $5M to $50M in the IBBA survey ([IBBA](https://www.ibba.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/mp-highlights-q2-2026.pdf)) and 7.0x in GF Data's sample of private equity-backed deals ([ACG](https://www.acg.org/news-trends/news/gf-data-reports-show-steady-middle-market-deal-flow-amid-more-selective)). Cleaner companies sell better too: [RSM US](https://www.rsmus.com/insights/services/business-tax/how-owners-drive-business-value) points to depth of leadership, a spread of customers, recurring earnings and reliable reporting. So my readiness list is short: monthly books clean enough for the next buyer's QoE, a manager who could run things for a month without me, no customer big enough to sink a year, more recurring work, and written processes.

How long is a typical hold? Bain puts the private equity average at exit at about 7 years, up from 5 to 6 in 2010 to 2021 ([Bain & Company](https://www.bain.com/insights/outlook-gaining-traction-global-private-equity-report-2026/)). PitchBook data reported by The New York Times counted 33,575 unsold private equity-backed companies on 30 June 2026, up from 15,923 a decade earlier, with buyers and sellers still far apart on price ("Private Equity Is Stuck With 33,575 Unsold Businesses", 10 August 2026) <!-- src:nyt-unsold -->. PitchBook data in The Wall Street Journal shows nearly 24% of private equity-backed companies had been held at least 7 years, where firms once aimed for 3 to 5 ([The Wall Street Journal](https://www.wsj.com/pro/private-equity/buyout-firms-look-to-swap-out-portfolio-company-ceos-as-holds-lengthen-7906e3e2)). So I treat 5 to 7 years as a plan, not a promise.

<div class="simple" markdown="1">
**The simple version.** Fixing up a house to sell isn't only paint. A new roof and tidy paperwork make it easier for the next buyer's bank to say yes. Bigger, cleaner and easier to finance sells for more.
</div>

<details class="deep" markdown="1">
<summary>For searchers and investors: how the returns are computed</summary>

Sale price = exit multiple x year 6 EBITDA. Proceeds = sale price minus the SBA balance minus the seller note with 6% a year of interest. MOIC = proceeds / the buyer's $213K. IRR uses one cash outflow at closing and the proceeds in year 6. The model assumes every spare dollar after the loan payment goes back into the business, so returns count sale proceeds only, and it ignores the SBA guaranty fee, deal costs and taxes on the sale. Illustrative, not a forecast.

</details>

## What people are saying {#sentiment}

**The news is about private equity's debt and AI roll-ups, and the online voices I read are wary of big private equity and warmer toward small owner-operators who keep the people.** I read the Financial Times and The Wall Street Journal, plus The New York Times and The Economist through a university library, and public posts on X, Hacker News and Reddit (Reddit only through search results).

**The news.** Debt dominates. About $140B of below-investment-grade loans (loans to riskier, lower-rated companies), mostly private equity-backed, come due in 2028 and $160B in 2029, against about $60B in a typical 2-year stretch from 2023 to 2025, per PitchBook data in The Wall Street Journal; Bain's Hugh MacArthur warned of "keys tossed back to the bank" ([The Wall Street Journal](https://www.wsj.com/finance/investing/a-300-billion-debt-wall-is-looming-over-private-equity-backed-companies-d6359497)). The 10-year Treasury yield hit its highest level since 2002 and companies rated CCC, a very risky grade, paid about 17% ([Financial Times](https://www.ft.com/content/7c7ccb82-2973-47af-ad9b-b469c6ea0048)). Fitch counts a 6.3% default rate in private credit, a 3-year high, mostly loans whose terms were changed rather than missed payments ([Financial Times](https://www.ft.com/content/04c5abf0-d7c4-47ca-9391-4a154c14e178)).

**Hacker News**, a forum for engineers and founders, barely mentions search funds: since 1 January 2025, 1 story on "search fund" (2 points) and no AI roll-up story above 8 points ([Hacker News search](https://hn.algolia.com/api/v1/search?query=%22search%20fund%22&tags=story&numericFilters=created_at_i%3E1735689600)). A thread on the debt wall leaned heavily negative ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50260526)). I saw no stories of debt stress.
